# The West Quay and Wren Shipwright

This is one small, authored waterfront composition for the clean artistic staging
world. R1 was placed and photographed; the real review found its repair boat
looked like a flat-roofed stall. R2 is a source revision awaiting another native
review. It does not reuse the rejected port's water-house arrangement.

The actual fresh 4 m native profile contains 169 samples across
`X1184..1232, Z-1276..-1228`. The bank faces west and bends east at the north end.
Water occupies the proposed pier area, while the compact workshop footprint is
on the southern dry bank. This is why the quay is not a straight north-facing
wall across the whole street.

The arrival walk joins the street's west end at `(1224,65,-1242)`. This plan stops
at X1223. An irregular low stone quay turns toward a lower westward finger pier,
with a small hammerhead and a wider crane apron. Six piles use exact actual
ocean-floor soundings and start in the first air/water cell above the natural
floor. The tool does not replace seabed cells.

The shipwright is a small seven-by-ten workshop with a cropped corner, timber
framing, an asymmetric roof with a repaired seam, paired doors and a partial
loft. Crafting and repair benches, tool storage, a private bunk and the ship model
have specific places. Nearby, an unfinished open boat rests on repair trestles;
its exposed frames and partial strakes are deliberate. A small cargo shade,
labelled supplies, fishing bench, casks, lighting and mooring furniture give the
working spaces a purpose without filling the passages with crates.

The timber crane has an operator platform, supported ladder, actual native
crank/shaft/pulley blocks and a solid headstock. It is stopped and uncommissioned.
No decorative chain, static crate, physics ship or item is claimed as a working
hoist. Load movement, collision, retention, safe operation and restart behavior
must be commissioned separately after its actual graphical design is reviewed.

The complete source is in `tools/artistic_harbor.py`. It checks native registry
properties, the adjacent street exclusion, fresh-profile block entities, planned
two-high walking routes and the owner's 3/9 material rule for every whole
structure. Distinct materials follow board lengths, crossbeams, masonry joints,
roof seams and timber roles. Facing changes do not count as material breaks.
The absolute nine-block cap is also checked across the entire combined harbor,
including every outdoor paving, retaining-wall and pier-deck position. Ownership
or structure boundaries cannot hide a long uninterrupted run.

The profile is coarse. Four of the workshop's 68 footprint cells have exact
samples; the other cells have nearby dry-land samples and remain explicitly
pending per-cell native admission. No interpolation is presented as proof that
a building is on dry land. Before placement, the host must inspect every actual
foundation cell and every prior-state target, and reject any wet, steep, altered,
inventory-bearing or otherwise conflicting location. Quay-edge support estimates
also require native inspection. This is a staging proposal, not a blanket
permission to fill a shoreline or river channel.

The export is restricted to an explicitly owned artistic staging home with an
owner-bound verified backup, an exact hash-bound fresh native profile, and its
current native registry. It writes a unique private folder, never a generic
`plan.json`, with a finite 16 MiB combined limit. A storage reserve check is
required first; that monitoring does not constrain unrelated applications.

The host independently read all 68 workshop columns from the exact stopped R1
backup and found natural dry ground with zero wet cells. Its private receipt is
`logs/harbor-exact-dry-foundations.json` (07:20 UTC). The exact 1,082-target native
preimage also passed before R1 placement. These results correct the earlier
coarse-profile uncertainty for that particular footprint; they do not prove all
later targets or award graphical/functional acceptance.

The genuine R1 exterior, pier and workroom photographs were inspected. Solid
stair rows made the unfinished boat read as a heavy roof, and isolated frame
tips looked disconnected. R2 replaces it with a fine-ended twelve-station boat,
an open cavity, connected U frames, thin partial strakes and thin trestle heads.
The tool bench, stopped native saw and timber rack form a clearer repair job.
The workshop uses the pinned Macaw roof model's thin fascia and correct ascending
direction, replacing the heavy reversed vanilla stair edge. These are visual
hypotheses until the actual R2 photographs are reviewed.

Revision exports bind the exact immutable R1 plan and hash and preserve its
files. They include an explicit expected-before view and bounded native delta;
removed old ribs, roof parts and planks are accounted for rather than left as
debris. All three serialized plans plus the manifest must fit the 16 MiB bound.

Six actual capture poses cover the quay/street junction, pier, workshop outside,
workroom, loft and repair ways. Genuine shader screenshots, support/lighting
inspection and native walking decide the next revision. No screenshot mockup or
source-only pass can substitute for that review.

Historical context comes from the [Royal Museums Greenwich depiction of Deptford
in 1775](https://www.rmg.co.uk/collections/objects/rmgc-object-108241),
[Chatham's dockyard buildings and ropery](https://thedockyard.co.uk/the-collections/historic-buildings/),
and the later comparative [Mystic Seaport ship chandlery](https://mysticseaport.org/explore/village-old/ship-chandlery/).
These inform the relationship of tools, stores and waterside work. The geometry
and composition are original; no museum artwork or downloaded schematic is used.
